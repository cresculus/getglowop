01 Example · 40 laptops

Ship the batteries. Keep the laptops.

A 40-laptop office was about to buy replacements at $1,100 each. The agent had been reporting battery health. The report named 40 batteries, not 40 dead machines. Example prices, not a quote.

  1. 1

    Collect →

    The agent on each PC reports the device. Age, battery, disk, last check-in. That is the data the subscription pays for.

  2. 2

    The pile →

    You ask for the repair list, or you hand the report over. Same 40 serials either way. Model, serial, and the part.

  3. 3

    Bulk →

    Those 40 go to a repair partner in one shipment or one drop-off. The shop replaces the batteries and sends the laptops back.

  4. 4

    The bill ✓

    40 replacements would have been $44,000. Forty batteries at $200 is $8,000. The office does not buy the 40 laptops.

$36k

not spent on replacements in this example. 40 × $1,100, minus 40 × $200 for the batteries.

02 More of the same

Same path. Different pile.

Disks · 18 desktops Swap the drive. Keep the tower. The report flags 18 failing disks and healthy chassis. One drop-off. The partner fits the drives. Eighteen new desktops at $900 would be $16,200. Eighteen drives at $180 is $3,240.
Mixed · 80 devices Only eight get replaced. 50 stay. 20 batteries go to the shop in one shipment. 8 boards are done, so those 8 are purchased. 2 have not checked in for months, so they get found before anyone buys a stand-in. The calendar had 28 purchases. The purchase order is 8.
Two offices Two drop-offs, one list. 30 laptops at the main office, 12 at the branch. The repair list splits by site. Each pile is dropped at a partner shop. Both offices skip the replacement order for those machines.
